Why strength comes first

Science: Muscle-strengthening activity is associated with roughly 10–17% lower risk of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease and cancer, with the largest benefit at about 30–60 minutes per week. That is almost exactly the dose of Option A.

The WHO recommends muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days a week for all adults, alongside 150–300 minutes of moderate activity (sport, brisk walking and cycling all count) and less sitting.

Strength is also a vital sign: in about 140,000 adults across 17 countries, lower grip strength predicted all-cause and cardiovascular death.

Expected benefit: A stronger, more injury-resistant body now, and a bigger reserve of muscle to age with.

Built for low motivation

  1. Never miss twice. One missed session is noise. In habit research, missing a single day didn't meaningfully slow habit formation, which took a median of about 66 days to become automatic.
  2. The two-exercise deal. On low days, promise yourself only the warm-up and the first two starred (★) exercises. If you stop there, it still counts.
  3. Decide when and where in advance. “If-then” plans have a medium-to-large effect on actually following through.
  4. Tick the box. Every completed session is logged in My week. Visible progress keeps you coming back.

Why full-body, 2–3 days a week

Why: Every muscle and movement pattern gets trained at least twice a week without long sessions.

Science: The ACSM recommends training every major muscle group at least twice a week. Training a muscle twice a week beats once a week for hypertrophy. For strength, results are similar across frequencies once weekly volume is matched, so the best frequency is the one you'll actually keep.

Expected benefit: Fewer, fuller sessions, which suits someone who isn't highly motivated.

Why bodyweight and 5 kg is enough for now

Why: Beginners don't need heavy loads. They need sets that are genuinely hard.

Science: Muscle growth is similar across light and heavy loads when sets are taken close to failure. Maximal strength favours heavier loads, which is why the program climbs to harder variations (single-leg, slow tempo, pauses) instead of adding endless reps.

Expected benefit: Months to years of progress with no equipment. Single-leg work roughly doubles the load on each leg.

Why so much single-leg work

Why: Walking, climbing stairs, running, cutting and lunging all happen on one leg.

Science: Unilateral training produces strength gains comparable to bilateral training, with similar transfer to sprint and change-of-direction performance. Single-leg squats and deadlifts are also among the highest-activation exercises for the gluteus medius.

Expected benefit: Strength that transfers to sport and daily life, better knee alignment, and fewer left-right imbalances.

Why effort is measured in reps in reserve

Why: “Stop with 1–3 reps left” makes each set productive without grinding to failure.

Science: Reps-in-reserve (RIR) scales are a valid way to set training intensity and adjust to day-to-day readiness. Training all the way to failure did not consistently improve outcomes for healthy adults, so stopping a little short loses nothing.

Expected benefit: Hard enough to drive adaptation, safe enough to repeat for years.

An honest note on upper-body shape

If a broader upper body (a "V-taper") is one of your goals: shoulder width (lateral deltoids) builds well here through pike push-ups and lateral raises. Lat development is the limiting factor without a pull-up bar: rows and prone pulls help, but at 5 kg they're a weaker stimulus. If it becomes a bigger priority, a doorframe pull-up bar is the single highest-return optional upgrade. Nothing in this program requires it.

Skills left out, and whyEquipment-free or 5 kg dumbbells only

    Before any advanced or elite skill

    • Meet the listed prerequisites first. They're a guide, not a guarantee of readiness: progress when the current step feels controlled for several sessions.
    • Skills that load the wrists (handstands, planche, L-sits): do the wrist preparation routine every session and stop for sharp wrist pain.
    • Inversions (wall walks, handstands, handstand push-ups) raise pressure in the head and eyes. Skip them with glaucoma, uncontrolled high blood pressure, recent eye surgery or if you feel dizziness or pressure behind the eyes.
    • Practise handstands away from furniture, glass and corners, and learn to bail out before you need to.
    • Skill work is best done fresh, early in a session, 2–3 times a week, stopping well before failure.

    Progressive calisthenics builds real strength: in a trial, a push-up progression improved bench-press strength much like bench pressing did.

    Know exactly when to make it harder

    One rule drives the whole program, double progression: earn the top of the rep range, then move up a rung.

    The progression algorithm

    1. Pick the right variation: one where you can reach the bottom of the rep range with clean form and 1–3 reps in reserve.
    2. Add reps: each session, try to add one rep to any set. Form first.
    3. Earn the upgrade: every set at the top of the range, with at least 1 rep in reserve, in 2 sessions in a row. Then climb one rung and restart at the bottom of the range.
    4. Downshift without ego: if you can't reach the bottom of the range, drop one rung.
    5. Add volume monthly: every 4 weeks, if recovery is good, add 1 set to 2–3 key exercises (maximum 4 sets each).
    6. Deload and retest: in week 8 and week 12, or earlier if fatigue signs pile up.

    This mirrors the ACSM approach of increasing difficulty once you can exceed the target reps in two consecutive sessions.

    The progression ladder

    Climb one rung at a time. Each rung makes the same exercise harder without new equipment.

    5Harder variation: split squat to reverse lunge, push-up to archer push-up
    4Add load: one or both 5 kg dumbbells
    3Add a pause: 2 seconds at the hardest point
    2Slow the lowering: 3–4 seconds down
    1Add reps: within the rep range

    When to add volume

    • Every 4 weeks, if you're sleeping 7+ hours most nights, soreness clears within 48 hours, and you completed at least 80% of sessions.
    • Add 1 set to 2–3 key exercises, not everything at once.
    • Cap at 4 sets per exercise and about 10–12 hard sets per muscle per week. Past that, choose harder variations rather than more sets.

    More weekly sets produce more muscle in a dose-response fashion, up to a point.

    Deloads

    • When: planned in weeks 8 and 12, or any time two or more fatigue signs persist for about a week, or a joint keeps grumbling.
    • How: 1 week, same exercises and variations, half the sets, about 4 reps in reserve. Keep doing Option D.

    Evidence on deloads is still developing. A 2024 trial found a 1-week deload didn't impair muscle gains, so use it to shed fatigue rather than as a ritual.

    When progress stalls

    A stall means no rep gain on an exercise for 3 sessions.

    1. Check the basics: sleep (7+ h), protein (about 1.6 g/kg/day), stress and illness.
    2. Film one set and fix form leaks: depth, tempo, knee path.
    3. For 3–4 weeks, swap to a sibling variation (split squat and reverse lunge) or a new rep range (6–10 instead of 12–20).
    4. Still stuck 2 weeks later: deload, restart one rung lower, and build back up.

    How to run each test

      Why progressive overload, and why gradually

      Why: The body only adapts to demands slightly above what it's used to. Without a rule for progressing, training stays comfortable and results stop.

      Science: The 2026 ACSM position stand found that the biggest gains come from simply doing resistance training regularly, with enough sets and effort; complex periodization added little. Steady, small progressions deliver that. At the same time, sudden spikes in training load relative to what you're conditioned for are a major injury risk factor, so each step up is small.

      Expected benefit: Steady, measurable progress with no guesswork, and fewer injuries from big jumps.

      Protect the joints that sport punishes

      This system is already built into every session. Here's what each piece does, why it's there, and how to spot trouble early.

      Knee

      ACL protection, hamstrings, landing and deceleration

      Neuromuscular programs that combine strength, balance, landing technique and feedback roughly halve ACL injury risk. No single exercise does it alone.

      Hamstring strains are among the most common soccer injuries, and the hamstrings help restrain the tibia against the forces that load the ACL. Programs that include the Nordic hamstring exercise cut hamstring injuries by about half.

      Knee alignment checklist
      • Knee tracks over the 2nd–3rd toe on every squat, lunge and landing.
      • Land soft and quiet: hips back, knees bent, chest over knees.
      • Decelerate over 2–3 short steps instead of one stiff plant when you can.
      • End the set when the knee starts caving. That rep rehearses the injury pattern.

      Ankle

      Sprain prevention, mobility, balance, foot strength

      Balance training reduces repeat ankle sprains. An unsupervised 8-week home program cut recurrences by about 35%, and both exercise therapy and bracing help prevent recurrence.

      Limited ankle dorsiflexion is linked to more knee valgus and less knee flexion when squatting, so ankle mobility also protects the knee.

      The small intrinsic foot muscles act as a “foot core” that stabilises the arch.

      If you've sprained an ankle before
      • Make the single-leg balance series a near-daily habit for at least 8 weeks.
      • Progress to eyes closed and unstable surfaces before adding hops.

      Hip

      Gluteus medius, groin, pelvic control

      The gluteus medius stops the pelvis dropping and the knee collapsing on one leg. Side-lying hip abduction, single-leg squats and single-leg deadlifts produce some of its highest activation.

      Groin: the Copenhagen adduction program reduced groin problems by 41% in male footballers and produced large gains in eccentric adductor strength.

      Long sitting shortens the hip flexors and switches off the glutes, so the hip-flexor stretch and bridges earn their place.

      Shoulder

      Rotator cuff, scapular control, long-term resilience

      Side-lying external rotation produces the highest infraspinatus and teres minor activity of common rotator-cuff exercises. These are the muscles that brake your arm after every throw, serve or racket stroke.

      Prone and side-lying scapular exercises preferentially train the lower and middle trapezius over the upper trapezius, which is the balance desk posture erodes.

      Scapular dyskinesis is common in overhead and racket athletes. It isn't always pathological, but scapular control training is a low-risk foundation.

      Rule of thumb
      • Each week, do at least as many pulling and shoulder-blade sets as pushing sets.

      Spine

      Core stability, anti-rotation, anti-extension, back-pain prevention

      Exercise, especially combined with education, is the only prevention strategy with good evidence for low back pain, lowering short-term risk by roughly 35–45%. Back belts and shoe insoles don't help.

      Core work here is endurance-based and spine-neutral, built on McGill's principles: anti-extension (dead bug), anti-lateral-flexion (side plank) and anti-rotation (bird dog, plank drag).

      The WHO advises limiting sedentary time and replacing it with activity of any intensity. A practical office target is a 1–2 minute movement break every 30–60 minutes.

      Sport-specific

      Field sports and racket sports

      Field sports (for example soccer). The main risks are hamstring strains, groin problems, ankle sprains and knee ligament injuries. Option D is modelled on the FIFA 11+, which cut injuries by about a third in young female players and by about 46% in male collegiate players. No hard leg work the day before a match. The day after, choose the upper-focused session, Option D or rest.

      Racket sports (for example table tennis, badminton, padel or tennis). Fast lateral footwork, explosive trunk rotation, repeated shoulder internal rotation on the forehand, and a constant semi-squat stance. Lateral lunges and deceleration sticks cover footwork, plank drags and open books cover rotation, and external rotations and Y-T-Ws cover the shoulder. Racket-sport-specific prevention trials are scarce, so these choices extrapolate from overhead-sport shoulder research and general injury-prevention evidence.

      Other sports: use the joint sections above that match your sport's demands.

      Pain during training: the referee system

      Some discomfort is normal while tissues adapt. Use this to decide whether to play on.

      Play on

      Pain 0–2 out of 10

      Train as planned. Mild muscle soreness 24–48 h after a session is expected.

      Yellow card

      Pain 3–5 out of 10

      Acceptable if it settles by the next morning and isn't creeping up week to week. Reduce range, tempo or load for that exercise.

      Red card

      Above 5, sharp, or worsening

      Stop that exercise and substitute a pain-free one. If it hasn't improved within 1–2 weeks of modifying, get it assessed.

      Stop and get assessed

      • A “pop” followed by rapid knee swelling, the knee giving way, or locking.
      • After an ankle or foot injury: unable to take 4 steps both straight after the injury and when you try again later, or bone tenderness along the back edge or tip of either ankle bone, at the base of the 5th metatarsal, or at the navicular (Ottawa ankle rules).
      • After a knee injury: unable to take 4 steps both straight after and later, unable to bend the knee to 90°, tenderness isolated to the kneecap, or tenderness at the head of the fibula (Ottawa knee rule).
      • Numbness, tingling or weakness spreading down an arm or leg.
      • Back pain with numbness around the groin or inner thighs (saddle area), new bladder or bowel problems, or weakness in both legs. These need same-day emergency care.
      • Back pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, constant night pain, or after a significant fall.
      • Chest pain, palpitations, fainting or unusual breathlessness during exercise. Stop immediately.

      Why this approach to mobility

      Why: Time is limited and motivation is precious, so mobility is folded into warm-ups and strength work rather than added as separate long sessions.

      Science: Strength training through a full range of motion improves range of motion about as much as stretching does, so the program itself is mobility work. Short static stretches (under 60 s per muscle) have only trivial effects on performance, and dynamic warm-ups are preferred before sport. Stretching alone hasn't been shown to reduce overall sports-injury rates, while strength training has.

      Expected benefit: Joints that keep their useful range into later life, with almost no extra time cost.

      Recovery is where you get stronger

      Training is the stimulus. Sleep, food and sensible scheduling are where the adaptation happens.

      Sleep

      • Adults should regularly sleep at least 7 hours. Treat 7 hours as the floor, not the target.
      • Keep a consistent wake time, within about 30 minutes, including weekends.
      • Stop caffeine by early afternoon: even 6 hours before bed, a large dose cut sleep by more than an hour.
      • Short sleep is linked to injury: adolescent athletes sleeping under 8 hours had about 1.7 times the injury risk.

      Fuel

      • Protein at about 1.6 g per kg of body weight per day (about 110 g for a 70 kg adult), spread over 3–4 meals. Gains plateau around this intake. If you have kidney disease, check your protein target with your doctor first.
      • If your weight and waist are already in a healthy range, eat around maintenance. Beginners can build muscle and lose a little fat at the same time (recomposition), so there's no need to diet hard while getting stronger.
      • Drink to thirst and eat a carbohydrate-containing meal 2–3 hours before a match or hard sport session.

      Weekly scheduling rules

      • At least 48 hours between strength sessions, since A and B are full-body.
      • No hard lower-body work in the 24 hours before a match.
      • The day after a match: the upper-focused session (B, Day 3), Option D, or rest.
      • Lighter skill sports (for example table tennis or golf) fit on any day. On a strength day, either order works.
      • Take one complete rest day if you feel run-down.
      • After time off, restart at about half volume. Sudden spikes in load raise injury risk.

      Signs of excessive fatigue

      • Performance dropping across 2 or more sessions despite effort
      • Soreness lasting beyond 72 hours, or heavy legs during sport
      • Poor sleep despite feeling tired, or a morning resting heart rate higher than your usual
      • Irritability, low mood or loss of motivation beyond your normal
      • Frequent colds or minor illness

      Two or more signs for about a week: take a deload week now. Persistent symptoms despite rest deserve a medical review.

      Injury warning signs

      • Pain that changes how you move, walk or land
      • Joint swelling, or pain that is sharp rather than achy
      • Pain that is worse the next morning, or creeping up week to week
      • Any of the “stop and get assessed” red flags
      Open the referee system

      Returning after a break

      • Under 1 week off: resume as planned.
      • 1–3 weeks off: one week at about half the sets, then normal.
      • Over 3 weeks off: drop one rung on the main exercises and rebuild over 2 weeks.

      Building back gradually avoids the load spikes that most often precede injury.
